Anaphylaxis — Recognition & Immediate Response

One protocol for anaphylaxis in this practice. Replaces the single-line "give epinephrine" references in other SOPs — this document owns that dosing and response.

Recognition

Anaphylaxis can develop within minutes of exposure to any product used in this practice — neuromodulator, filler, topical anesthetic, vitamin or peptide injection — and can worsen quickly after appearing mild. Treat any of the following as anaphylaxis until proven otherwise.

SystemSigns
Skin / MucosalHives, flushing, generalized itching, swelling of lips, tongue, or face
RespiratoryDifficult or noisy breathing, wheeze, persistent cough, hoarse voice, throat tightness or swelling, difficulty talking
CardiovascularPersistent dizziness, fainting or near-fainting, rapid or weak pulse, pale and floppy appearance, collapse
GastrointestinalSudden nausea, vomiting, cramping, or diarrhea, especially combined with any sign above

One System Can Be Enough

Skin symptoms plus any respiratory or cardiovascular sign together are treated as anaphylaxis without waiting for the picture to worsen.

Respiratory or cardiovascular collapse alone, even without a rash, is treated as anaphylaxis — skin signs are absent in a meaningful minority of cases.

Do not wait for the patient to deteriorate further to confirm the diagnosis. Treat on the systems above, not on certainty.

Immediate Action — In Order, In Parallel

Multiple Staff Act At The Same Time

With more than one staff member present, these steps happen simultaneously — one person gives epinephrine while another calls 911, rather than working through a list end to end.

If only one staff member is present, epinephrine comes before the 911 call. Give the injection, then call.

  1. STOP. If anaphylaxis develops during a procedure, stop the injection, stop the application, remove the causative product from contact with the skin if topical. This is the first thing that happens here, unlike generic bystander first aid.
  2. GIVE EPINEPHRINE. Do not wait to see if symptoms resolve, and do not give an antihistamine first — see What Not To Do. This is the single most time-critical action on this page.
  3. CALL 911. State the location precisely — which practice address, room number. Happens at the same time as step 2 where staffing allows.
  4. POSITION THE PATIENT. Supine with legs elevated, unless breathing is difficult — then allow the patient to sit with legs outstretched. If unconscious, recovery position. If pregnant, position on the left side.
  5. REASSESS. Airway, breathing, circulation. Begin CPR at any point if the patient becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally.
  6. NOTIFY. The medical director or on-duty covering provider, and the patient's emergency contact.

Epinephrine Dosing

Confirm What This Practice Stocks

Complete against the specific product and concentration actually stocked at each location before this protocol is relied on in an event.

FormDoseSiteNotes
Auto-injector (e.g. EpiPen)0.3 mg, fixed adult doseMid-outer thigh, through clothing if necessaryHold per device instructions, ~3 seconds; note the time given
Manually drawn, 1:1000 (1 mg/mL)0.3–0.5 mg IM (0.3–0.5 mL)Vastus lateralis — mid-outer thighIntramuscular only, never IV at this concentration

Repeat every 5–15 minutes if there is no improvement, or symptoms return. Most patients respond to a single dose; some need a second.

Intramuscular in the thigh, not the arm or buttock — faster, more reliable absorption. Note the exact time of every dose given.

What Not To Do

Antihistamines & Steroids Are Adjuncts, Never First

Diphenhydramine, cetirizine, and similar antihistamines do not treat the airway or cardiovascular components of anaphylaxis and do not substitute for epinephrine under any circumstance.

Corticosteroids act too slowly to matter in the acute phase.

Both may be given after epinephrine, if ordered by the treating provider — never in place of the injection, and never first.

  • Do not allow the patient to stand or walk
  • Do not wait for a second sign or symptom before treating if the first is already respiratory or cardiovascular
  • Do not delay the epinephrine dose to ask whether the patient carries a personal auto-injector — use what the practice stocks

Transport & Handoff

Every patient who received epinephrine is transported to emergency care, even if symptoms fully resolve on-site. Not optional, not a judgment call left to the patient.

At handoff to EMS, provide:

  • What was given — medication, dose, route, exact time
  • The suspected trigger — product name and lot number where applicable
  • Symptom timeline — onset, progression, response to treatment
  • Any relevant medical history disclosed by the patient

Biphasic Reaction

Symptoms Can Return Hours Later

A biphasic reaction — recurrence of anaphylaxis after apparent resolution, without re-exposure — can occur up to 72 hours later, most commonly within 8–10 hours.

This is exactly why every patient goes to emergency care rather than being monitored in-office and sent home once symptoms settle.

Advise the patient in writing before they leave: seek immediate care again if any symptom returns, even hours later, even if the first episode felt fully resolved.

Post-Event

  • Do not resume the procedure that triggered the reaction
  • Document the suspected trigger clearly in the chart and flag it prominently for future visits
  • Refer to an allergist for formal evaluation before any future treatment with the same or a related product category
  • Report per EMA-COMP-002, Medical Error and Adverse Event Reporting, regardless of outcome

Documentation

  • Time of onset and every symptom observed, by system
  • Suspected trigger, including product name and lot number
  • Every dose of epinephrine given — time, dose, route, site
  • Time 911 called and time EMS arrived
  • Positioning and any additional intervention — CPR, oxygen
  • Transport destination and receiving provider, where known
  • Biphasic reaction warning provided in writing

Readiness — Before It Happens

  • Check epinephrine expiration dates monthly, at every location. An expired dose discovered mid-event is entirely preventable.
  • Confirm every treatment room has immediate access to the practice's epinephrine supply — not locked away, not in a different room
  • Quarterly drills practicing recognition and the parallel-action sequence above
  • All clinical staff trained at onboarding and annually, regardless of whether they are authorized to administer epinephrine themselves
